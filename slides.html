The Time Cost of Food

Country-level time and energy conversion factors
for food provisioning across 187 countries


Data year: 2020  ·  Preliminary

Why time?

Food systems are assessed in calories, carbon, and water — but rarely in human time.

What we know
Greenhouse gas emissions, water footprints, and land use are routinely tracked across global food supply chains.
What's missing
How many hours does it take to put 2,000 kcal on the table — including cooking, growing, and all upstream labor?
This paper: Compute a time conversion factor — hours per 2,000 kcal and per 100 g protein — for every country, decomposed into domestic production and trade-embedded components.

Research questions

  • How much total human time (economic + non-economic) does food provisioning require per capita?
  • How does this vary across the income spectrum — from low-income to high-income countries?
  • Is there a time–energy tradeoff? Do countries that save time spend more energy?
  • How much of a country's food-time footprint is embedded in imports?
  • Does the picture differ by gender?

Three components of food time

1
Household (non-economic) time
Cooking, food preparation, home growing, foraging — from Global Human Day / Time Use Surveys, split by gender.
2
Direct labor in food sectors (economic)
Hours embedded in agricultural and food-processing supply chains — via EXIOBASE3 labor satellites mapped through FABIO.
3
Indirect labor in non-food sectors (economic)
Hours in packaging, transport, energy, and other upstream industries supporting food — derived via Leontief inverse.

The bridging problem

FABIO is mass-based (tonnes). EXIOBASE is monetary (USD). Connecting them requires:

Step 1 — Mass → EXIO sectors
Split FABIO mass vectors into EXIO product categories using monetary shares within each concordance row. Produces a 23,001 × 37,400 mapping matrix.
Step 2 — Intensities → per tonne
Convert EXIO labor/energy intensities from per-USD to per-tonne, then reorder from 49 EXIO regions to 187 FABIO countries.
Step 3 — Leontief propagation
Non-food sector intensities flow through the full economy via L = (I − A)⁻¹, capturing upstream indirect effects.

Trade decomposition

Each country's footprint is split into three parts:

D
Domestic — time embodied in food produced and consumed at home
E
Export — time a country's workers spend producing food for other countries
I
Import — time embodied in food a country consumes but did not produce

Both food-sector (direct) and non-food-sector (indirect) components are decomposed separately — for energy, male labor, and female labor.

Illustrative findings (preliminary)

  • Household (non-economic) time dominates in low-income countries — often >80% of total food time.
  • Economic labor dominates in high-income countries, but is mostly invisible to consumers.
  • A time–energy tradeoff is visible: labor-saving food systems tend to be more energy-intensive.
  • Trade embeds substantial time — some countries import more food-time than they produce.
  • Female time burden is consistently higher than male across all income groups, driven by household work.

The time–energy tradeoff

Low-income countries
  • High household labor time
  • Low mechanization → low energy use
  • Large female–male gap
High-income countries
  • Low household time (outsourced to industry)
  • High energy footprint per kcal
  • Smaller gender gap
Implication: Reducing household time burden requires either energy-intensive industrialization or structural changes to food systems (infrastructure, markets, gender norms). There is no free lunch.

Country spotlight structure

For each country we produce two figures:

Stacked bars: domestic + export (positive), import (negative), by gender and sector. Allows comparison of production-based vs. consumption-based footprints.
Continental trade flows
Where does the country's food-time come from and go to? Broken down by continent of origin/destination.

Interactive Sankey diagrams show commodity-level trade flows for kcal and protein at continental resolution.
